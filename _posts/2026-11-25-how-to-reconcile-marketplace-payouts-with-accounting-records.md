<p>Marketplace payouts rarely match your bank deposits dollar for dollar, and if you&#39;re closing your books off the deposit amount alone, you&#39;re misstating revenue every single cycle. This guide walks through the exact steps to reconcile marketplace payouts with your accounting records, including where sellers lose track of fees, refunds, and reserves.</p>

<p>A marketplace payout is not revenue. It&#39;s revenue minus referral fees, FBA fees, advertising spend, refunds, chargebacks, and sometimes a reserve holdback the platform won&#39;t release for weeks. When you book the deposit as sales, your P&amp;L overstates margin and your tax filings understate real revenue — both problems auditors flag immediately.</p>
<p>Multichannel sellers feel this worse than single-channel stores. Amazon settles every 14 days, eBay deposits through Managed Payments in 1-2 business days per sale, Etsy runs its own deposit schedule based on shop history, and Walmart Marketplace works on yet another calendar. Stack five payout rhythms against one monthly close and the mismatch compounds fast. By 2026, most sellers running three or more channels are dealing with this exact bottleneck, and it&#39;s the single most common reason a bookkeeper flags unexplained variance at month-end.</p>

<h2>The steps</h2>
<h3>1. Pull the itemized settlement report, not the summary</h3>
<p>Every marketplace summary total hides the line items you need. Amazon&#39;s Payments report under Reports &gt; Payments breaks down gross sales, referral fees, FBA fees, refunds, and reserve amounts by settlement period. Walmart Seller Center and eBay Seller Hub have equivalent detailed exports.</p>
<p>Common mistake: reconciling off the deposit amount that lands in your bank account. That number is already net of every fee — you can&#39;t back into the fee breakdown from it without the itemized report.</p>
<h3>2. Separate gross sales from deductions in a working spreadsheet</h3>
<p>Build three columns minimum: gross sales, total fees (referral, fulfillment, advertising), and net payout. This mirrors how your accounting software should record the transaction — gross revenue booked separately from an expense line for marketplace fees.</p>
<p>Expected outcome: your gross sales column should tie to your order management data almost exactly, since gross sales reflects units sold, not units paid out.</p>
<h3>3. Match settlement periods to your accounting calendar</h3>
<p>Amazon&#39;s 14-day settlement cycle rarely lines up with a calendar month. A settlement period that starts March 24 and ends April 6 straddles two accounting months, so you need to prorate or accrue the mid-period portion.</p>
<p>Most sellers handle this with an accrual entry: book revenue and fees as they occur (order date), then reconcile the actual settlement deposit against that accrual when it lands. This keeps your monthly P&amp;L accurate even when payout timing doesn&#39;t cooperate.</p>
<h3>4. Reconcile refunds and chargebacks as their own line</h3>
<p>Refunds don&#39;t just reduce revenue — they often carry a partial fee refund that differs from the original fee charged. A $40 refund on Amazon might return only $36 of the original referral fee, leaving a $4 gap that needs its own expense entry.</p>
<p>Common mistake: netting refunds against gross sales in one line. Keep refunds separate so you can spot patterns — a spike in refund-driven fee loss usually points to a listing or fulfillment problem worth fixing, not just a bookkeeping nuisance.</p>
<h3>5. Account for reserves and holdbacks separately</h3>
<p>New sellers and high-return categories often hit a reserve — funds the marketplace withholds for 7-21 days beyond the standard settlement period. That money is real revenue you&#39;ve earned but haven&#39;t received, so it belongs on your balance sheet as a receivable, not as unrecorded income you forget about.</p>
<p>Expected outcome: your reserve balance should decrease as older holds release and increase as new orders trigger fresh holds. If it only grows, something in your account health or return rate needs attention.</p>
<h3>6. Post the reconciled entries to your accounting software</h3>
<p>Once gross sales, fees, refunds, and reserves are separated, post them as distinct line items in QuickBooks or Xero: one entry for gross sales, one for the marketplace fee expense, one for refunds, one for the reserve receivable. This is the entry structure that survives a tax review without follow-up questions.</p>
<h3>7. Confirm the bank deposit matches your net payout total</h3>
<p>The final check: the actual deposit hitting your bank account should equal gross sales minus all fees, refunds, and reserve holds for that settlement period. If it doesn&#39;t match within a few dollars, you&#39;ve missed a line item — usually advertising spend deducted directly from the payout or a currency conversion fee on cross-border sales.</p>

<p><strong>The deposit total doesn&#39;t match my gross sales minus fees.</strong>
Check for advertising spend deducted directly from the payout rather than billed separately — Amazon and Walmart both do this, and it&#39;s the most common source of unexplained variance.</p>
<p><strong>My reserve balance keeps growing month over month.</strong>
This usually signals a rising return rate or an account health flag. Pull your return rate for the last 30-60 days and compare it against your category average before assuming it&#39;s a bookkeeping error.</p>
<p><strong>Refund amounts in the settlement report don&#39;t match what I issued.</strong>
Marketplaces often refund fees at a different rate than they charged them, especially on partial refunds. Book the difference as a separate small expense line rather than forcing the numbers to match.</p>
<p><strong>Two settlement periods overlap a single accounting month and my accrual entry is off.</strong>
Recalculate the accrual using order-date revenue for the days inside that month, not settlement-date revenue. Settlement dates almost never align with calendar boundaries.</p>
<p><strong>Currency conversion fees are eating into international payouts.</strong>
Cross-border sales on Amazon and eBay often carry a conversion fee separate from referral fees. Add a dedicated expense line for currency conversion so it doesn&#39;t get buried in miscellaneous fees.</p>
<p><strong>Reconciliation takes hours longer on multichannel accounts than single-channel ones.</strong>
That&#39;s expected when you&#39;re pulling five separate reports manually. Consolidating order, fee, and payout data through one platform — which is exactly what SellerChamp does for multichannel sellers — cuts the manual export step out of the process entirely.</p>
